# Product Specification

## Product goal

Scriber is a native macOS menu-bar dictation app intended as a direct replacement for Wispr Flow.

- Remain available from the menu bar.
- Menu-bar presence is user-configurable and enabled by default. Removing the item through macOS updates the same Settings preference; windows, launch-at-login, and global shortcuts continue independently.
- Show the Dock icon while a normal Scriber window is open. “Show in Dock” is user-configurable and disabled by default; disabling it never closes a visible window. When disabled, closing the final normal window with Command-W, Command-Shift-W, or the red window control removes Scriber from the Dock while menu-bar and dictation services continue. When enabled, Scriber remains in the Dock and app switcher without an open window.
- Save local dictation history.
- Group Dictation history by local calendar date and vertically center each entry's time beside its transcript content. Keep the page centred, with one transparent continuously rounded card per day, a subtle outline, and row separators that span the card's full width. Separate one day's card from the next by a gap wide enough to mark the boundary on its own, since no label sits between them.
- Name the day currently at the top of Dictation history in a strip inside the window's titlebar, below the toolbar items, hanging 2pt off the cards' leading edge, the way a Finder sidebar heading sits a little left of the rows beneath it — the heading reads as sitting above the card rather than inside it, and the content is the only thing that looks indented. It is the only place a day is named; the list itself carries no day label. Give the strip no backdrop of its own — it shares the titlebar's, and a separate surface beneath the toolbar cannot be made to match it. Hand the name over to the next day as that day's card reaches the titlebar, and collapse the strip whenever no day is on screen.
- Where a Settings tab has an untitled group it leads, and holds what the tab would contain if it had no other groups. Such a group is optional: a tab may begin with a titled one instead. A title covers every card beneath it until the next title, so an untitled card following a titled one continues that group rather than starting a nameless new one. An action belonging to the whole tab rather than to any one group sits below the last card, trailing-aligned, with no card of its own, and scrolls with the content; an action scoped to one group stays a row inside it.
- A divider between Settings rows means one setting has ended and the next has begun, and nothing else. A sentence explaining a single control belongs inside that control's row and is set at the size AppKit draws its own row captions; a note covering a whole group belongs under that group's title, above the card, a step larger — the order the system already puts them in; a setting and the setting that depends on it share one row, the dependant indented and disabled with its parent. Nothing that is not a setting of its own may sit in a row of its own — a caption, a warning, a meter, or the controls that drive one all read as separate settings the moment a divider is drawn above them.
- Scroll Dictation history under the window's titlebar, which shows a separator only while content sits beneath it.
- Confirm a Dictation-history copy with a brief toast in the window's bottom-right corner, without changing the row's copy control.
- Insert each completed transcript into the text cursor that is focused when transcription completes, not the one that was focused when recording began. The user may move focus while transcription runs, and delivery follows that final cursor.
- If insertion cannot be confirmed, preserve the transcript in Dictation history, copy it when appropriate, and present recovery actions in the floating pill.

Versioning follows the repository-wide [`VERSIONING.md`](VERSIONING.md).

## Identity and workspace boundary

- The product name is **Scriber**, the app bundle is `Scriber.app`, and the native bundle identifier is `com.gafiegarcia.scriber`.
- The native identity was a deliberate clean reset from Scriber Dictate. Do not migrate its history, preferences, onboarding state, pending audio, login item, or Keychain item into Scriber.
- The app exposes a **Dictation** workspace and a separate **Settings** window. Do not add an empty Transcription workspace before that workflow exists.
- Settings presents five tabs — General, Dictation, Sound, ElevenLabs, Permissions. The window is a fixed 660x520 and cannot be resized, so every tab is seen at the size it was designed at; a pane taller than that scrolls, and the window is never sized to whichever tab has the most in it. A route that opens Settings to fix something selects the tab that owns the problem; opening Settings without naming one leaves the tab the user last chose alone. Match the Settings window by scene identifier before title: a tabbed pane may retitle its window after the selected tab, and fronting, Command-Shift-W, and the Dock activation policy all depend on recognising it.
- `Escape` closes the Settings window, unless a shortcut recorder is capturing — where it ends the capture — or a confirmation is presented, which answers it itself.
- The main window has no sidebar. It owns one persistent SwiftUI toolbar carrying the workspace control, the dictation count, any unresolved recovery condition, and search. The window title is not displayed.
- The workspace control and the count form one leading group: the count is the workspace's subtitle, and adjacency is what keeps it reading as one. The recovery warning comes last of all, so that a condition appearing or clearing grows into the space before the search field instead of moving a control the user aims at. The warning is not part of the workspace group: it reports that the app cannot run, which is not a fact about the active workspace.
- Declare the search field with `DefaultToolbarItem(kind: .search)` and do not also ask `.searchable` for `.toolbar` placement, or the toolbar gets two of each. `.searchable` anchors the field to the trailing edge behind a flexible space of its own, and no toolbar placement or declaration order gets past it.
- SwiftUI owns that toolbar alone. Never replace `window.toolbar`, never hide or remove a SwiftUI-created toolbar item from AppKit, and never vary a toolbar item's shared-background preference by state: each one makes SwiftUI reconcile chrome it still holds observers on, and the first crashed on launch.
- The workspace control names the active workspace while Dictation is the only one, and becomes a switcher when a second exists. Do not ship a picker that offers a single choice. While it is a plain name it carries no background; it earns one when it becomes a control.
- The toolbar search filters the active workspace in place, and Command-F focuses it. Both are unavailable in the Settings window.
- Presenting the main window gives the toolbar search keyboard focus, so the first thing typed into a window that just opened searches. Merely returning to a window that stayed open must leave focus, selection, and scroll position alone.
- Future long-form **Transcription** is a separate workspace with its own model, source-media policy, metadata, editing, export, and note lifecycle. Keep `DictationRecord` focused on short capture, delivery, and retry.
- Reuse credentials, `ScribeClient`, language, usage, and keyterm behavior only through real shared boundaries. Split coordinator responsibilities when the Transcription workflow is implemented.

## Shortcuts and job lifecycle

- The shortcut is chosen from presets or recorded, in one control used by both Settings and setup. Presets and the recorded chord are one choice, not two, so no divider separates them and the active one is tinted with the accent colour. Every preset is a key held on its own — `fn`, `Right ⌘`, `Right ⌥` — because a key held alone is the only trigger with nothing to learn and nothing to collide with. The last recorded chord is kept when a preset is chosen, so switching back does not lose it.
- One configurable shortcut serves both dictation modes, `fn` by default. Recording begins on the press, before the mode is known, so nothing waits on the distinction. Released within the tap threshold, the recording carries on with the user's hands free and the same shortcut pressed again stops it; released after the threshold, it stops there, which is holding to talk. A stored chord that is no longer bindable is replaced with the default when it loads.
- Refuse a binding that macOS or the foreground app already answers to: any chord whose only modifier is Command, and the system combinations for screenshots, Mission Control, switching spaces, Spotlight and input sources, the character viewer, the Dock, locking, logging out, force quit, full screen, zoom and the other accessibility bindings, keyboard navigation, display mirroring, Help, and the text-editing keys every macOS text field honours. The tap swallows what it matches, so a binding taken from one of these replaces it everywhere, not just in Scriber. `⌘⇧D` remains bindable. Also refuse a single modifier held on its own — Shift types capitals and the others each open most shortcuts on the system — except the keys `ReservedShortcuts.bindableAlone` names: `fn`, which macOS gives no role of its own, and `Right ⌘` and `Right ⌥`, which no shortcut on the system begins with — the left twin is the one every hand reaches for. Refusing a left twin says the right one is free, because that refusal is where someone finds out.
- A refused chord says why, and ends the capture exactly as `Escape` does. The reason stays on screen until the next recording begins or the Settings window closes, whichever comes first — it explains a key that was just pressed, and the guard that produced it does not need restating later. No rejection leaves a recorder listening.
- Display `fn` before Control, Option, Shift, and Command whenever it is part of a multi-modifier shortcut label.
- While the binding is being configured, all existing global shortcut matching is suspended without removing the Accessibility event tap. Only one shortcut recorder may listen at a time, recognized keys are displayed live, and modifier-only chords preserve the largest combination that was actually held simultaneously.
- The Accessibility event tap sits in front of every keystroke on the system, and consumes only the configured dictation chord and `Escape` while a pill is showing. Every other key reaches the foreground app unchanged and in its normal position in the event stream, never withheld and resent. The tap decides whether to consume a key while that key is passing, because the decision is what gates the event, and it does nothing else there: every resulting action runs on the next main-loop turn, in the order the keys arrived. Work done inside the tap delays the user's own typing everywhere.
- A keyed chord ends its hold when any of its modifiers is released, not only when its key comes up, so a recording never outlives the chord that started it.
- A shortcut bound to particular modifier keys answers to those keys alone, and ends when any one of them comes up. `Right ⌘+Right ⌥` is a different shortcut from `Left ⌘+Left ⌥` and from one of each, and the same rule holds wherever a shortcut is matched — the global tap and setup's own test of it both. Neither the modifier flags nor the event's report of them carry a side; only the key code on the event that changed them does, so which keys are down has to be tracked as they arrive. A twin held down keeps the flag set, which is why a release read from the flags alone would never arrive.
- Only `⌘` and `⌥` carry sides, and only in a chord built entirely from them with no ordinary key. Both have a right-hand key on every Mac keyboard and neither is typed right-handed to open a shortcut; `⌃` often has no right key at all and right `⇧` types capitals all day, so naming their sides would invent a distinction the keyboard does not make. Everything else is stored unsided and answers to either hand, including anything stored before sides existed.
- A modifier-only chord containing `⌘` is refused unless every key in it is a right-hand one. `⌘` with another modifier is how most shortcuts on the Mac begin, so binding it starts a recording on the way into `⌘⌥I` or `⌘⇧4`. Held on the right it is clear of all of them, which is the same reason a lone `Right ⌘` is allowed.
- During the first second of a held recording, any non-modifier key cancels and discards it, counted from the press rather than from the microphone opening. `fn`+delete is forward delete, so it starts a dictation on the way past and has to end it at once; a window at the start that does not yet accept the key is a window in which that fails. Hands-free is never cancelled this way — once a dictation is locked, typing is just typing. The dictation chord is not typing, and neither is its auto-repeat: a chord held down produces one press and one release, and only a fresh press starts the next recording.
- `Escape` cancels either recording mode. Cancelled recordings retain retryable audio only when they are at least one second long and contain detected speech; shorter or silent cancellations are discarded. This one-second recovery rule does not replace the configured signal threshold for normally completed recordings. The recovery pill can undo cancellation and resume transcription plus automatic insertion, while History retry transcribes and copies the result without inserting it.
- Opening a Scriber window changes nothing about a dictation, whichever route opened it — Command-comma, the menu bar item, or the toolbar's warning control. A recording in flight carries on, because delivery reads the frontmost app when the transcript is ready rather than when the window opens, so a trip into Scriber and back loses nothing and a recording ends only when the user asks for it to; and a notice about a dictation that already ended stays up, because a route that is not the notice's own action must not throw away the recovery it offers. Only the pill's own controls retire it: taking the action a notice offers is what answers it, and the window owns what happens next. No control may change the dictation shortcut while a dictation is running: the presets, the recorded chord, and **Record** are disabled for as long as one does, in setup as well as in Settings. Any other control that cannot act while a dictation runs is disabled for the same span, rather than refusing a click in silence.
- **“No sound from the microphone”** belongs to a dictation someone actually gave, so it requires the same one second the recovery rule uses. A shorter recording that carried no signal closes the way a brief press does, in silence: below that length it describes a burst of taps or a slipped finger, and reports silence to someone who never spoke.
- A press too brief to have been a dictation ends in silence: no sound, no message, no history entry, no retained audio, just the pill closing. This threshold sits below the one-second recovery rule, so nothing dropped here could have been kept for a retry, and it governs every way out of a recording that short — released, cancelled by a keystroke, or failed inside the capture stack. `fn` is both the default binding and the modifier half of other people's shortcuts, so a press Scriber was never meant to receive must not answer back.
- Only one recording or transcription job runs at a time.
- A press arriving while the previous recording is still being closed starts a new recording rather than being refused. Presses arrive faster than the capture stack reports a recording finishing, and a recording that is never reported must not leave the recorder unable to start for the rest of the session.
- A notice about a dictation that already ended — a failure, a cancellation, a copied result, a permission or credential block, or “No words detected” — never blocks the next one. Both shortcuts start recording immediately from any such state, whether or not its pill is still on screen. Only an in-flight recording or transcription may refuse a start.
- Maximum recording duration is 10 minutes.

## Recording and transcription

- Scriber's input settings must let the user see the microphone working and reach the volume that governs it. A level meter runs only when asked, so opening the tab never opens the microphone, and it closes with the tab, the window, and the app. While it runs, point a flat level at the input volume in macOS Sound Settings and offer the way there — the picker cannot show or change that volume, and a meter reading flat is otherwise a dead end. Name what to check rather than what Scriber does not control: someone watching a flat meter wants the next step, not the boundary between the app and the system.
- The sounds Scriber plays while dictating are enabled by default and configurable as one setting, under Sound. Play the built-in macOS Blow sound on the press that starts a dictation, before the capture session has opened and never conditional on what the input device is — pressing the shortcut has to answer at the same speed whatever is plugged in, and a cue that waits for the microphone is a cue that arrives a second late on a Bluetooth headset. The user's own system alert sound once for a terminal recording or transcription failure, and Tink once when recording is cancelled or automatic paste falls back to a copied transcript. The failure cue is the system alert sound so it matches what the user already recognises as an error, and it also covers the two microphone outcomes — no signal at all, and signal with no words — because both are terminal and both are easy to miss on screen alone. Silencing the system alert sound silences this cue with it. Retry waits remain silent. A press too brief to have been a dictation plays no cue of its own, and a start cue still sounding when one ends fades out rather than stopping dead — cutting a cue mid-attack is itself a sound, and on a built-in speaker still powering up it is the loudest one Scriber can make.
- Muting other app audio while dictating is off until the user turns it on, and is offered on setup's last step alongside Launch at login. A private Core Audio process tap silences all audio except Scriber's while playback continues; destroy the tap as soon as capture stops or is cancelled. Never pause or resume another app, and never read, inspect, log, or persist tap audio.
- Failure to create the other-audio mute tap must never prevent dictation. Keep recording unmuted and expose the unavailable state on the Sound tab in Settings.
- Use ElevenLabs Scribe v2 batch transcription with no secondary rewrite model. “Remove filler words and false starts” controls `no_verbatim`, defaults on, and remains user-configurable.
- Include personal keyterms after validating them against Scribe limits. Return adds the keyterm in the field.
- A key with Speech-to-Text access remains verified and usable when its account-usage scope is unavailable. Keep any cached credit usage as visibly subdued last-known information with its update time, expose one retry action, and never let cached exhaustion block a verified key whose current usage cannot be read.
- Retry transient failures up to three total attempts, waiting 3 seconds and then 5 seconds.
- Delete audio only after a successful transcript has been saved.
- Retain failed or interrupted audio so the job can be retried.
- Do not create a history entry or spend API credit for recordings that never cross the configured signal threshold. Report the rejection rather than discarding it silently, and distinguish it from a recording that did carry sound but produced no words: a microphone that is muted, at zero input volume, or simply the wrong device must not look identical to not having spoken.
- A microphone that sends healthy audio and then stops partway through delivers a transcript of what was captured, with nothing indicating the rest is missing. Do not add a warning for it. Bluetooth input arrives over a gated call path that emits literal digital silence between phrases, so any detector built on a trailing silence fires on ordinary pauses instead. A microphone that never sends anything at all is still reported.
- Treat empty, whitespace-only, and punctuation-only successful responses as “No words detected,” clean up their temporary record and audio, and do not offer a meaningless retry.

## Delivery and floating pill

The current delivery transaction and its regression baseline are defined in [`PASTE_ENGINE.md`](PASTE_ENGINE.md).

- Starting a recording must never send an Accessibility message. Accessibility calls are synchronous cross-process requests whose cost is controlled by the destination app, so target discovery belongs to delivery time only. Recording start may resolve the pill's screen from the window server, which does not traverse another app's Accessibility tree.
- Confirming delivery is explicitly allowed to be slow. Waiting several seconds for a destination to request the promised transcript is correct; reporting a false failure is not.
- Show a floating pill at the bottom center of the active screen while recording, transcribing, and reporting terminal states.
- While holding a recording, show Cancel on the pill's leading edge only while the pointer is over the pill, widening and narrowing the pill to match; Escape remains the always-available way to cancel without hovering. Show both Cancel and Confirm unconditionally, with no hover requirement, once a recording is locked into hands-free. Converting a held recording to hands-free widens the existing pill and animates only Confirm into it, since Cancel is either already showing or one hover away. Cancel uses the normal recording-cancellation path and Confirm stops and transcribes.
- Preserve the previous clipboard after confirmed automatic insertion.
- Mark the temporary promised-text pasteboard item as transient, concealed, and autogenerated so compatible clipboard-history tools do not consume it or expose private in-flight dictation. Treat a request for that concealed text as operational confirmation that the foreground destination handled the paste; observable Accessibility mutation may also provide positive confirmation, but missing Accessibility evidence must never turn a successful opaque-editor paste into failure.
- If the foreground destination does not request the promised text before the bounded timeout and no observable mutation occurs, keep the transcript in Dictation history, republish it as ordinary clipboard text, and present the copied-result recovery UI. Merely dispatching a Paste command is never confirmation.
- Recovery UI may offer See History, Undo, Retry, Check Input, Review, Update Key, or View Credits according to the failure state. One destination keeps one name across every pill that reaches it, and that name matches the section it lands on.
- Tint the pill by what the outcome was, from the same tone vocabulary the window's toast stack uses, so the two surfaces cannot colour one outcome two ways. A dictation in flight is not an outcome and carries no tint, and neither does a cancellation: the user asked for it, so warning them about it would be the app disagreeing with a deliberate choice. Tint, never fill: the pill floats over the user's own work and must still read as glass.
- A success and a warning must be tellable apart at a glance in both light and dark appearance. Light glass washes an accent out, so the tint carries more weight there; equal strength in both appearances is a failure, not consistency.
- Light the pill's top and bottom edges so its glass reads as a lit edge rather than a flat panel. Neither `NSGlassEffectView` nor SwiftUI's `Glass` provides this, so Scriber draws it.
- Every pill phase has an explicit whole-pill click. It may open a window or a Settings pane and nothing else: no click on the pill body may transcribe, spend API credit, cancel a recording, or discard an offered recovery. Phases whose controls own the interaction, and phases presenting selectable text, take no body click at all.
- The pill must not activate Scriber merely by appearing. Actions that do not open an app window should preserve the foreground app; window-opening actions should intentionally activate Scriber.
- `Escape` or the close control dismisses the visible pill according to its current phase without deleting saved history or retained retry audio.

## Persistence and security

- Store Dictation history locally with SwiftData until the user manually deletes it.
- Confirm before deleting history, whether one entry or all of it. A transcript has no undo and no trash, and every deletion route — the entry's overflow menu, its context menu, and Clear Dictation History — must ask first.
- Retained audio from failed or cancelled dictations expires after 30 days. This is user-configurable and enabled by default. Expiry removes only the recording: the history entry, its transcript, and why it failed are always preserved, and the entry reports that it can no longer be retried. Audio that no dictation references is removed on the same schedule.
- Keep Scriber's SwiftData history in its dedicated `Scriber/History.store`; never use the generic Application Support `default.store` shared by unsandboxed apps.
- Store the API key in the default encrypted macOS login Keychain using modern `SecItem` APIs, accessible only while unlocked and only on this device. Never store the key in a plaintext file or `UserDefaults`.
- Keep the storage policy capable of returning to the Data Protection Keychain. Moving there would fence the item into the app's own access group, so no other app could prompt the user for it; it costs an entitlement and an embedded provisioning profile, which is why it is not the default. Any such build must prefer and migrate the current login-Keychain value before considering an older protected item.
- Never log, export, or persist the key elsewhere.
- Diagnostic logging carries only what Scriber observed about itself: permission values and the path that saw them, paste-target identities and roles, and window state. Never a transcript, never audio, never the key. Each category writes a line only when something changes, so a quiet log means nothing moved rather than nothing was watched.
- Validate credentials without uploading audio or consuming transcription credit before saving them.
- Normal automated tests must not access production credentials, contact ElevenLabs, or consume API credit.

## Permissions and app lifecycle

- Microphone permission is required for recording.
- Accessibility permission is required for global shortcut interception and cross-app insertion.
- Every unmet permission offers exactly one action, labelled `Allow`, worded the same in Settings and in onboarding. The title never reports what macOS has recorded so far, and one click raises one thing: the system prompt where that prompt can still grant the permission, the matching System Settings pane otherwise. A permission already granted offers one action too, named for the System Settings pane it opens, so a grant can be reviewed or withdrawn; each names a different destination, because buttons sharing a name announce alike to VoiceOver. No button may be shown that cannot do the thing it names.
- The system-audio usage description exists solely for optional recording-time muting. Scriber drives its private mute tap with a private aggregate device and an IOProc whose callback discards the buffers untouched; it never inspects, copies, records, or saves system-audio samples.
- Say in Scriber's own interface that muting works whether the grant is allowed or declined, and why: Scriber never reads what other apps play, so the permission protects nothing it wants. This is a mechanism rather than a coincidence — other apps go silent while the prompt is still on screen, before any answer and while Scriber has no entry in that pane at all, so the mute never depended on the grant to begin with. What the grant governs is the audio content, which Scriber discards. Keep that out of `NSAudioCaptureUsageDescription`, which macOS renders inside its own security dialog and which no build can correct once shown — the behaviour is undocumented, and a promise there would be unfixable if Apple ever enforced it. Do not claim that being listed is what makes it work: only the declined case has been measured, and the first attempt is what creates the listing.
- Keep the mute on a process tap, and do not run a voice-processing unit beside the recorder for any reason. Ducking (`kAUVoiceIOProperty_OtherAudioDuckingConfiguration`) needs no System Audio Recording grant and ramps the level where a tap can only switch, which makes it look like the better mechanism twice over — it is not, and the second reason is fatal. Ducking lives on the voice side of the unit, so it requires input enabled, and a voice unit holding the built-in microphone starves the recorder's own capture of it: flat waveform, and a transcript cut off at the point the recording started. It survives a Bluetooth input, whatever device Settings names, which is exactly what lets it look like it works. It is also unable to exclude the app applying it, so Scriber's own start cue lifts the duck for as long as it plays. A tap excludes Scriber by process object and bundle identifier and touches no input at all, which is what makes it the right one.
- Nothing reports whether System Audio Recording was granted. `CGPreflightScreenCaptureAccess` answers for the screen-capture list, which Scriber is not in, and returns false whether the grant was given or refused; Core Audio ships no preflight for process taps at all. A refusal therefore cannot be detected, and must not be designed around — say what the grant is for and leave the answer to macOS.
- A mute that fails to start is not reported: the audio that failed to stop is playing, and a warning about it names something the user is already hearing. A mute that fails to end is reported, because a Mac that stays silent has no visible cause and a fix nobody would guess.
- Other apps get their sound back a moment after a dictation ends rather than in the same instant, which is when a device changing mode is heard as a glitch. Quitting Scriber and turning the setting off both restore immediately instead: one is going away and cannot honour a delay, and the other is an instruction about right now.
- Recording-time muting stays off until the user turns it on, and turning it on asks macOS for the grant immediately. No dialog stands in front of that request: Scriber's explanation is `NSAudioCaptureUsageDescription`, which macOS shows inside its own prompt, where it sits on the question it answers instead of a click earlier. Muting works whether the grant is given or refused — confirmed on macOS 26 and 27 — so a refusal is not a failure and nothing reports it as one. Core Audio ships no preflight for process taps, so attempting one is the only thing that raises the prompt; the attempt runs off the main thread, because macOS blocks the caller while its prompt is up. Left to the first dictation the prompt arrives with the shortcut still held down, owing an answer before anything else can move. Scriber cannot read whether System Audio Recording is granted — Core Audio ships no preflight, unlike the microphone, Accessibility and screen capture — so the only way to raise that prompt is to attempt a tap. A default-on setting therefore spends the prompt during a first dictation, on a permission whose name claims far more than the feature does. Explain it in one place shared by every surface offering the toggle.
- After onboarding, missing or revoked Microphone or Accessibility permission must be visible immediately in the Dictation window and menu bar. Scriber must present an actionable permission pill on launch, when a grant is revoked, and when it can observe an attempted dictation; the pill and the window's warning control both route to Scriber Settings.
- Unresolved permission and credential conditions appear together in the main window's chrome, never auto-dismiss, and leave only when resolved. That is what licenses the floating pill presenting one recovery at a time. A condition is never a toast: toasts are transient by contract and carry outcomes, not state.
- Present an unchanged missing-permission state at most once per launch. Merely focusing a Scriber window refreshes permission state without presenting the same pill again or restarting its dismissal timer; a later revocation, changed missing-permission set, or attempted dictation may present it again.
- Accessibility revocation prevents Scriber from observing the global shortcut itself, so Scriber must monitor permission state independently, stop unavailable shortcut monitoring, and restart it automatically when the grant returns. It must never rely on the blocked keypress as the only warning path.
- Launch at login is optional, offered during onboarding as a checkbox on its final step, defaults on for a first run, and requires explicit consent. Ticking or clearing it acts at once, so an answer macOS refuses arrives where the request was made rather than on the way out. A box nobody touches is applied when setup ends or its window closes — whichever comes first — so a window closed early still honours the default rather than dropping it. A Redo Setup offers whatever macOS currently has.
- The Launch at login setting shows what macOS has registered, not what Scriber last asked for, and follows a change made in System Settings on the same refresh that tracks permissions. When macOS holds the entry switched off under Background App Activity, Scriber cannot turn it back on. Asking for it anyway, in Settings or on setup's last step, must explain the refusal, name that list, and route to Login Items; the control drops its claim rather than standing ticked over a message that contradicts it. The explanation belongs to the refused request alone: switching Scriber off there is a decision the user is entitled to make, so the state on its own says nothing.
- A launch macOS makes at login opens no window, takes no activation, and leaves the front app alone: menu bar, shortcuts, and dictation come up on their own. "Start in the background" in Settings controls this, defaults on, and is offered only while Launch at login is on. It governs login launches alone — a launch the user makes presents its window however that route normally would, and setup still opens whenever it is incomplete. "Show in Dock" is honoured either way.
- The onboarding window must open centred and fully visible on the current display, whether it is the first launch or a Redo Setup request made with the main window already open. It is a fixed size the scene derives from its content, never the height of the screen. AppKit's own frame is not trusted for this: a cascaded frame put it under the Dock, and a restored one brought it back too small.
- Setup is one decision to a step, with a step's own gate the only thing standing between it and the next. Nothing may appear on a step that is not that decision. Each step reads live state, so a Redo Setup shows what is already granted as done rather than asking for it again.
- A step gates only on what it exists to establish: a key that verified, a microphone that was actually heard, a granted permission, a shortcut that was actually pressed. The microphone step carries a way past that is present from the first second and reads as troubleshooting, because nothing Scriber can read distinguishes a Mac with no microphone from one whose input is silent — virtual devices list exactly like real ones.
- Setup teaches hands-free dictation before holding, names the second mode in the same breath as the first, and never leaves either to be discovered in Settings.
- Setup tells the user that ElevenLabs trains on new accounts' recordings by default, where the switch is, and that turning it off governs only what is sent afterwards.
- Setup is complete on reaching its dictation step, which is what lets that step be a real dictation: the shortcut tap refuses to run until then. Every decision has already been made by that point, so closing the window there leaves setup finished rather than half-done.
- Every launch presents onboarding until setup is complete, then presents the main Dictation window, except for a login launch that starts in the background. Closing the final normal window still leaves menu-bar and dictation services running.
- Onboarding must be complete and the credential definitively usable before recording or Dictation retry can begin.
- App Sandbox remains disabled while global event interception and cross-app Accessibility insertion are core behavior.

## Platform and release boundary

- Native Swift 6.4 app using SwiftUI, AppKit, SwiftData, AVFoundation, Accessibility, and Keychain APIs.
- Toolchain baseline: Xcode 27 beta with Swift 6.4 until a later explicit toolchain decision.
- Supported target: Apple silicon and macOS 26 or later. Treat 26 as a floor the code sets, not a version chosen for convenience: the pill and the toasts are built on `NSGlassEffectView` and SwiftUI's `.glassEffect`, and the muting service reads `isProcessRestoreEnabled` and `bundleIDs` on its process tap, all of which are macOS 26 only. Lowering the floor therefore means designing and maintaining a second appearance for everything the user sees while dictating, plus a fallback for muting — a parallel design rather than a build setting. Do not attempt it as part of some other change.
- Release builds sign with a Developer ID Application certificate under the hardened runtime, carrying the audio-input entitlement and no provisioning profile. The certificate's private-key backup remains outside the repository. Every released build is notarized and stapled.
- Scriber reports that a newer release exists and never installs one. Check at most once a day, on launch and on demand, and let the user switch the check off. Persist the answer: a check that runs daily would otherwise drop an offer every time the app restarts. Order versions numerically rather than as text, which ranks `0.10.0` below `0.9.0` and would silently stop offering updates. Surface an offer in the menu bar menu and in Settings, never in the menu bar icon, which reports configuration problems alone.
- A real ElevenLabs smoke test is always explicit and opt-in.
